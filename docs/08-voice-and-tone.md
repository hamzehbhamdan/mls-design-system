# Voice and tone

> **Sourcing note.** MLS has not published a league-level public style guide. Everything below is either observed consistently across MLS's own output, or drawn from Atlanta United's published club style guide — which is unusually thorough and covers league-wide terminology, but is *a club's* guide, not the league's. Where a rule comes from Atlanta rather than MLS, this chapter says so. Do not present club guidance to stakeholders as league policy.

## Three registers, not one voice

MLS runs three clearly separable registers. They are not variations in formality — the vocabulary, sentence length and stance toward the reader genuinely differ. Using the wrong one is the most common way brand copy goes wrong.

### Register A — League communications

Formal, dateline-driven, actively voiced, sponsor-integrated. Near-zero humour. Short-to-medium sentences with heavy proper-noun density.

Dateline format is fixed: city, then day-of-week plus full date in parentheses, then a dash.

```
NEW YORK (Wednesday, January 21, 2026) –
```

The standard opening puts the adverb before the verb:

> Major League Soccer **today announced** three substantial roster rule modifications

Disciplinary releases go passive and clinical, deliberately avoiding an agent for the punishment:

> Matt Miazga's previously announced three-game suspension will be reduced by one game following a successful petition based on his continued commitment and compliance with a recommended treatment program

Executives are attributed with full title before name: *MLS Executive Vice President Player Strategy and Relations Todd Durbin.*

### Register B — Editorial (mlssoccer.com)

Short sentences, active voice, present-tense energy, and a genuine willingness to be funny and self-deprecating. Hype-forward, but not humourless about the hype — it will undercut itself.

Recaps are compact and verb-driven:

> Myrto Uzuni wasted little time putting on a show for Austin FC… converting a perfectly placed free kick… completing a blistering first-half hat trick

Peak moments go full crescendo — all caps, exclamation, emoji, unembarrassed mythologising, and the British trophy-lifting idiom rather than "win":

> CHAMPIONS! Inter Miami & Lionel Messi win MLS Cup over Vancouver Whitecaps
> Inter Miami CF **have climbed the mountaintop**
> 🎊 INTER MIAMI LIFT THE TROPHY 🎊

Columns and Power Rankings are loosest — second person, ellipsis, fragments, dry humour at the league's own expense:

> The Casemiro experience so far has been… well, interesting.
> **MLS gravity** is pulling them back to the middle of the pack in the West.

"MLS gravity" is a good example of in-house shorthand that assumes an initiated reader. **MLS writes to people who already follow it, not to newcomers.**

### Register C — Social

Fragments, emoji as punctuation, looser proofreading standard. The league's own YouTube bio is the cleanest artefact:

> Welcome to the official YouTube page of Major League Soccer! MLS features stars from around the world, including one 🐐 from Argentina. **We score bangers only.** Watch them here.

Four sentences, three under eight words, one emoji doing lexical work unglossed, British-inflected slang.

Platform rules from Atlanta United's published guide, which is the clearest picture of how a club in this ecosystem is instructed to write socially:

- Do not end a post with a bare period or a bare emoji
- Maximum three hashtags
- Never write the club name in body copy — use the hashtag form
- Never tag players or staff in a negative context
- **"Avoid tagging supporters or supporter groups, unless doing activation or account takeover"**

That last rule is the most transferable: **supporters' groups are treated as autonomous entities the club does not casually appropriate.**

## The rule that matters most

**Hype attaches to moments, never to the league's own standing.**

Recaps and finals get maximal language — "climbed the mountaintop," "monumental." But when MLS talks about *itself competitively*, it becomes markedly plain, even self-critical:

> We've got to win Concacaf consistently. We've got to get into the Club World Cup, and get out of the early rounds.

The league's own framing separates commercial success (achieved) from being one of the world's top leagues athletically (aspirational, openly unfinished).

**Celebrate the match. Be honest about the league.** Copy that inverts this — understated about a final, boastful about the league's global standing — is off-brand in a way that is immediately obvious to the audience even if they cannot name it.

## Fans and supporters are not synonyms

Two-tier and deliberate.

| Term | Means | Used in |
|---|---|---|
| **Fans** | The general audience | Operational, legal, commercial and conduct contexts. The Fan Code of Conduct uses "fans" exclusively — never "supporters." |
| **Supporters** | The organised, committed constituency | Institutional, ceremonial and respectful contexts. The commissioner's letter opens *"To our supporters across the United States and Canada: thank you."* |

**Use *supporters* when acknowledging, honouring or addressing. Use *fans* when describing an audience, ticketholders or conduct rules.** Swapping them reads as tone-deaf in both directions.

## Supporter culture

MLS's posture is celebratory, credit-giving, and diplomatically vague about friction. The league frames supporter culture as **something supporters built and the league benefits from**, not something it created:

> the trophy was created by the fans … Now it's what the league markets, what makes MLS stand apart.

Tension is acknowledged euphemistically — "hiccups along the way", "inconsistencies in terms of how away supporters were allowed to support" — and resolution is framed as education between the league, clubs, supporters and security.

**A caution.** MLS's relationship with supporters' groups is genuinely contested territory. The 2019 political-display dispute produced significant public conflict, which is why the Fan Code of Conduct contains explicit language about "electioneering, campaigning or advocating." Brand language in this area is politically loaded, not neutral. Write with awareness of that; do not adopt a breezy tone about it.

Vocabulary, all lowercase and unglossed: **tifo**, **capo**, **supporters' group**, **away support**. MLS itself is inconsistent between *supporters' group* and *supporters group*; this system standardises on the apostrophe, to match *Supporters' Shield*.

## Loanwords

**Permissive.** MLS editorial uses Spanish and global-football vocabulary untranslated and unitalicised: *golazo*, *brace*, *clean sheet*, *side*, *kit*, *bangers*, *tifo*, *capo*, *clásico*.

> Eddie Segura & LAFC stun Toluca with stoppage-time **golazo**

Do not italicise them. Do not gloss them. Doing either signals that you are explaining the sport to an outsider, which is the opposite of the register.

Club nicknames work as second-reference variety, capitalised as proper nouns: *the Black & Gold*, *the Verde & Black*, *the Five Stripes*, *the Loons*, *the Revs*, *the Quakes*.

## Quick tonal test

| Off-brand | On-brand | Why |
|---|---|---|
| "MLS is one of the world's premier leagues." | "We've got to win Concacaf consistently." | The brand does not claim standing it has not earned. |
| "Fans are invited to celebrate with us." | "To our supporters: thank you." | Wrong tier. |
| "The team is playing well." | "The Timbers **are** playing well." | Club names take plural verbs. |
| "an impressive two-goal performance" | "a brace" | The audience is initiated. |
| "The franchise announced…" | "The club announced…" | "Franchise" is prohibited. |
| "Portland won the trophy." | "Portland lift the trophy." | Peak-moment idiom. |
